2929: \begin{definition}
2930: \label{definition-essentially-constant-diagram}
2931: Let $M : \mathcal{I} \to \mathcal{C}$ be a diagram in a category
2932: $\mathcal{C}$.
2933: \begin{enumerate}
2934: \item Assume the index category $\mathcal{I}$ is filtered and
2935: let $(X, \{M_i \to X\}_i)$ be a cocone for $M$, see
2936: Remark \ref{remark-cones-and-cocones}. We say $M$ is
2937: {\it essentially constant} with {\it value} $X$ if there exist an
2938: $i \in \mathcal{I}$ and a morphism $X \to M_i$ such that
2939: \begin{enumerate}
2940: \item $X \to M_i \to X$ is $\text{id}_X$, and
2941: \item for all $j$ there exist $k$ and morphisms $i \to k$ and $j \to k$
2942: such that the morphism $M_j \to M_k$ equals the composition
2943: $M_j \to X \to M_i \to M_k$.
2944: \end{enumerate}
2945: \item Assume the index category $\mathcal{I}$ is cofiltered and let
2946: $(X, \{X \to M_i\}_i)$ be a cone for $M$, see
2947: Remark \ref{remark-cones-and-cocones}. We say
2948: $M$ is {\it essentially constant} with {\it value} $X$ if
2949: there exist an $i \in \mathcal{I}$
2950: and a morphism $M_i \to X$ such that
2951: \begin{enumerate}
2952: \item $X \to M_i \to X$ is $\text{id}_X$, and
2953: \item for all $j$ there exist $k$ and morphisms $k \to i$ and $k \to j$
2954: such that the morphism $M_k \to M_j$ equals the composition
2955: $M_k \to M_i \to X \to M_j$.
2956: \end{enumerate}
2957: \end{enumerate}
2958: Please keep in mind Lemma \ref{lemma-essentially-constant-is-limit-colimit}
2959: when using this definition.
2960: \end{definition}
2961: 
2962: \noindent
2963: Which of the two versions is meant will be clear from context. If there is
2964: any confusion we will distinguish between these by saying that the first
2965: version means $M$ is essentially constant as an {\it ind-object}, and in
2966: the second case we will say it is essentially constant as a {\it pro-object}.
2967: This terminology is further explained in
2968: Remarks \ref{remark-ind-category} and \ref{remark-pro-category}.
2969: In fact we will often use the terminology ``essentially constant system''
2970: which formally speaking is only defined for systems over directed sets.

2972: \begin{definition}
2973: \label{definition-essentially-constant-system}
2974: Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a category. A directed system
2975: $(M_i, f_{ii'})$ is an {\it essentially constant system}
2976: if $M$ viewed as a functor $I \to \mathcal{C}$
2977: defines an essentially constant diagram. A directed inverse system
2978: $(M_i, f_{ii'})$ is an {\it essentially constant inverse system} if
2979: $M$ viewed as a functor $I^{opp} \to \mathcal{C}$ defines an
2980: essentially constant inverse diagram.
2981: \end{definition}

3286: \begin{lemma}
3287: \label{lemma-characterize-essentially-constant-ind}
3288: Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a category. Let $M : \mathcal{I} \to \mathcal{C}$
3289: be a diagram with filtered index category $\mathcal{I}$.
3290: The following are equivalent
3291: \begin{enumerate}
3292: \item $M$ is an essentially constant ind-object,
3293: \item there exists a cocone $(X, \{M_i \to X\}_i)$ such that for any
3294: $W$ in $\mathcal{C}$ the map
3295: $\colim_i \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, M_i) \to \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, X)$
3296: is bijective,
3297: \item $X = \colim_i M_i$ exists and for any $W$ in $\mathcal{C}$
3298: the map
3299: $\colim_i \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, M_i) \to \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, X)$
3300: is bijective, and
3301: \item there exists an $i$ in $\mathcal{I}$ and a morphism $X \to M_i$
3302: such that for any $W$ in $\mathcal{C}$ the map $\Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, X) \to
3303: \colim_{j \in \mathcal{I}} \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, M_j)$
3304: is bijective.
3305: \end{enumerate}
3306: In cases (2), (3), and (4) the value of the essentially constant system is $X$.
3307: \end{lemma}
3308: 
3309: \begin{proof}
3310: It is clear that (3) implies (2). Assume (2).
3311: Then $\text{id}_X \in \Mor_\mathcal{C}(X, X)$
3312: comes from a morphism $X \to M_i$ for some $i$, i.e., $X \to M_i \to X$
3313: is the identity. Then both maps
3314: $$
3315: \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, X)
3316: \longrightarrow
3317: \colim_i \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, M_i)
3318: \longrightarrow
3319: \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, X)
3320: $$
3321: are bijective for all $W$ where the first one is induced by the morphism
3322: $X \to M_i$ we found above, and the composition is the identity. This means
3323: that the composition
3324: $$
3325: \colim_i \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, M_i)
3326: \longrightarrow
3327: \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, X)
3328: \longrightarrow
3329: \colim_i \Mor_\mathcal{C}(W, M_i)
3330: $$
3331: is the identity too. Setting $W = M_j$ and starting with $\text{id}_{M_j}$
3332: in the colimit, we see that $M_j \to X \to M_i \to M_k$ is equal to
3333: $M_j \to M_k$ for some $k$ large enough. This proves (1) holds.
3334: 
3335: \medskip\noindent
3336: Assume (4). Let $k$ be an object of $\mathcal{I}$. Setting $W = M_k$
3337: we deduce there exists a unique morphism $M_k \to X$ such that
3338: there exists a $j$ and morphisms $k \to j$ and
3339: $i \to j$ in $\mathcal{I}$ such that $M_k \to X \to M_i \to M_j$
3340: is equal to $M_k \to M_j$. The uniqueness guarantees that we obtain a
3341: cocone $(X, \{M_k \to X\})$. In this way we see that (4) implies (2);
3342: some details omitted.
3343: 
3344: \medskip\noindent
3345: We omit the proof that (1) implies the other conditions.
3346: \end{proof}
